To extend your swap-coupling to continuous systems, replace the binary indicator $s_i \in \{0,1\}$ with a **continuous coupling function** $\sigma(t) \in [0,1]$. This gives you a one-parameter family of dynamics that interpolates between standard evolution and fully swapped evolution.

---

## 1. Continuous-Time Formulation

Let $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = (F_x(x,y), F_y(x,y))$ be a vector field. Define the **swap matrix**:

$$
S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The coupled dynamics with coupling function $\sigma(t) \in [0,1]$ are:

$$
\boxed{
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \Big[(1-\sigma(t))\, I + \sigma(t)\, S\Big]\, \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}(t))
}
$$

Written component-wise:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{x} &= (1-\sigma)\, F_x(x,y) + \sigma\, F_y(x,y), \\[4pt]
\dot{y} &= (1-\sigma)\, F_y(x,y) + \sigma\, F_x(x,y).
\end{aligned}
$$

### Special cases

| $\sigma(t)$ | Dynamics | Physical meaning |
|-------------|----------|------------------|
| $0$ | $\dot{x}=F_x,\ \dot{y}=F_y$ | Standard (uncoupled) |
| $1$ | $\dot{x}=F_y,\ \dot{y}=F_x$ | Fully swapped |
| $1/2$ | $\dot{x}=\dot{y}=\frac{F_x+F_y}{2}$ | Both coordinates driven by the average |
| $\in [0,1]$ smoothly | Interpolated | Partial coupling / cross-talk |

---

## 2. Coupling Coefficient for Continuous Time

The discrete empirical frequency $\nu_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N s_i$ becomes a **time-average**:

$$
\boxed{
C_T := \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \sigma(t)\, dt
}
$$

If $\sigma$ is constant, then $C = \sigma$ directly. If $\sigma(t)$ oscillates rapidly, $C_T$ captures the long-term average coupling strength.

---

## 3. Connection to Your Discrete Theory

Your discrete update rule:

$$
x_{i+1} = x_i + (1-s_i)\Delta x_i + s_i \Delta y_i
$$

is the **Euler forward discretization** of the continuous system above, with:

- Step size $\eta$
- $\Delta x_i = \eta F_x$, $\Delta y_i = \eta F_y$
- $\sigma(t_i) \approx s_i$

The smoothed discrete version (which you mentioned but couldn't find) simply replaces $s_i \in \{0,1\}$ with $\sigma_i \in [0,1]$:

$$
x_{i+1} = x_i + (1-\sigma_i)\Delta x_i + \sigma_i \Delta y_i.
$$

This is numerically better behaved because small changes in $\sigma$ produce small changes in the trajectory, whereas binary switching can introduce discontinuous jumps in the update direction.

---

## 4. How This Differs from Standard Coupling

| Feature | Standard Coupling | Your Swap Coupling |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Mechanism** | Add interaction terms: $\dot{x} = F_x + \alpha(y-x)$ | Redistribute existing components: $\dot{x} = (1-\sigma)F_x + \sigma F_y$ |
| **New information** | Introduces new physics (diffusion, feedback) | No new terms; only mixes existing ones |
| **Conservation** | May conserve quantities via added structure | Conserves $\dot{x}+\dot{y} = F_x+F_y$ for all $\sigma$ |
| **Geometric meaning** | Perturbs the vector field | Rotates/redistributes the vector field in component space |

**Key invariant:** For any $\sigma$,
$$
\dot{x} + \dot{y} = F_x + F_y.
$$
The sum of the rates is preserved; only the **allocation** between coordinates changes. This is fundamentally different from standard coupling, which modifies the total dynamics.

---

## 5. Potential Issues to Address in Your Paper

1. **Timescale mismatch.** If $F_x$ and $F_y$ have different natural scales (e.g., fast vs. slow dynamics), swapping them directly can destabilize the system. You may need a **scaling matrix** $D = \text{diag}(d_x, d_y)$:
   $$
   \dot{\mathbf{x}} = \big[(1-\sigma)I + \sigma S\big] D \mathbf{F}.
   $$

2. **Interpretation at $\sigma=1/2$.** Both coordinates evolve identically: $\dot{x}=\dot{y}$. This collapses the 2D system toward the diagonal $x=y$, which may or may not be physically meaningful.

3. **Not a standard tensor operation.** The swap matrix $S$ is coordinate-dependent. Under a rotation of the $(x,y)$ basis, $S$ does not transform covariantly. Your coupling is tied to the specific coordinate decomposition $(F_x, F_y)$, not to a geometrically invariant structure.

---

## 6. Suggested Formal Definition for Your Paper

> **Definition (Continuous Swap Coupling).** Let $\mathbf{F}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be a vector field with components $F_i$. A **swap coupling** with strength $\sigma \in [0,1]$ is the vector field $\mathbf{F}^{(\sigma)}$ defined by
> $$
> \mathbf{F}^{(\sigma)} = \big[(1-\sigma)I + \sigma P\big]\mathbf{F},
> $$
> where $P$ is a permutation matrix exchanging two (or more) components. The **coupling coefficient** over interval $[0,T]$ is $C_T = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \sigma(t)\,dt$.

This frames your idea clearly: you are not redefining the gradient, but defining a **family of coupled vector fields** parameterized by how much the components are cross-allocated.
